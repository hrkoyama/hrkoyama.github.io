Diagnosing numerical instability, oscillation, and divergence

Learning goal: Distinguish error amplification and divergence from stable oscillations and physical instability, and isolate their causes.

A visibly wavy solution is not enough to diagnose numerical instability. Stable linear methods can create oscillations or overshoots near sharp changes. Conversely, an initially small error can keep amplifying over time.

First measure the difference from a reference solution, then isolate its dependence on time, grid, step, and boundary treatment. Distinguish physical growth, numerical amplification, phase error, and lack of monotonicity.

Isolate the cause with four questions

Is the solution growing, or is its difference from the reference growing? Does reducing the time step change the behavior? Are only short grid wavelengths growing? Is error concentrated near boundaries? Changing one factor at a time makes the cause easier to trace.

Stability and monotonicity differ

Expanding the Lax-Wendroff stencil reveals a negative right-neighbor coefficient even for 0<ν<1. The new value can therefore leave the range of old values. L2 stability for a periodic linear problem differs from a maximum principle, positivity, and monotonicity.

Some unstable growth is physically correct

When the physical model amplifies perturbations, growing waves are not necessarily numerical errors. Compare analytic growth rates or independent references, and examine resolution and time-step dependence. This edition does not run a new benchmark of physical instability.

A small worked example

For a hand calculation, insert ν=0.6 and neighboring values (left,center,right)=(0,0,1) into Lax-Wendroff. The updated center is 0.6(0.6-1)/2=-0.12, demonstrating lack of monotonicity.

This one-step negative value differs from the FTCS norm growing over time in Chapter 5. The figure also shows a smooth wave's error at finite time.

nu = 0.6
left, center, right = 0.0, 0.0, 1.0
value = (nu*(1+nu)*left/2
         + (1-nu*nu)*center
         + nu*(nu-1)*right/2)
print(value)  # -0.12 by hand

The smooth-wave figure shows the difference between numerical and exact solutions on the finest adopted grid. It is not a long-time test of a discontinuous wave.

Review questions and answers

Question 1: Does overshoot necessarily imply L2 instability?

Show answer

No. Monotonicity and an L2 amplification bound are different properties.

Question 2: Should grid, step, and boundaries all be changed at once when diagnosing a problem?

Show answer

Start with one at a time. Otherwise an improvement may not reveal which change helped.

Common pitfalls

  • Do not equate stable oscillation with unbounded amplification.
  • Separate physically growing solutions from amplification of error.

Key points

  • Define what you mean by unstable.
  • Use time, wavenumber, and boundary dependence to locate error sources.
