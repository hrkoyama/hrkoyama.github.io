Numerical dissipation: amplitude, norms, and energy

Learning goal: Read artificial amplitude decay from the magnitude of the amplification factor, distinguish it from physical diffusion and dissipation, and understand the role of artificial viscosity.

Numerical dissipation is artificial wave-amplitude reduction introduced by discretization. For periodic advection without physical damping, the exact waveform translates unchanged, preserving its amplitude and L2 norm. Measure extra decay relative to that reference.

Amplitude decay in a diffusion equation, by contrast, can be correct physical behavior. For each problem, distinguish quantities that should be conserved, quantities that physically dissipate, and changes introduced by the numerical method.

Read the amplitude multiplier

A Fourier mode is multiplied in amplitude by |G| per step and by |G|ⁿ after n steps. A quantity proportional to amplitude has a different multiplier from an energy-like quantity proportional to amplitude squared. Where |G|=0, the logarithmic damping rate is not finite.

Artificial diffusion in the upwind scheme

For smooth solutions at fixed Courant number, a Taylor expansion makes the leading upwind error look like diffusion. Accounting for temporal as well as spatial truncation produces the factor 1-ν. This is a small-spacing approximation, not an exact description of every wavenumber.

Benefits and costs of dissipation

Dissipation can suppress unresolved oscillations, but can also smear or remove structures of interest. Stable linear first-order upwind preserves monotonicity at the cost of smearing the waveform. Less numerical dissipation is not automatically best in every situation.

A small worked example

Use the same advection speed, Courant number 0.6, and initial sum of two sine waves. Relate |G| in symbols.csv to the norm changes in advection.csv.

Upwind and Lax-Wendroff are both stable under these conditions, but damp high wavenumbers differently. Examine poorly resolved components as well as the leading low-wavenumber error.

from lab import symbol
from math import pi
for method in ["upwind", "lax-wendroff"]:
    G = symbol(method, pi/2, 0.6)
    print(method, abs(G), abs(G)**20)

Artificial dissipation weakens as the grid is refined, but the upwind method in this fixed sequence has first-order error. Check grid dependence before interpreting numerical decay as a physical diffusion coefficient.

Review questions and answers

Question 1: If amplitude is multiplied by 0.9, what happens to a quantity proportional to its square?

Show answer

It is multiplied by 0.81. Keep amplitude and energy-like multipliers distinct.

Question 2: Is every amplitude decrease in a diffusion equation numerical dissipation?

Show answer

No. Compare with the analytic physical decay, and examine the difference as numerical error.

Common pitfalls

  • Do not combine physical and artificial dissipation into one undifferentiated quantity.
  • Do not call a leading modified-equation term exact all the way to the grid limit.

Key points

  • Use |G| to read the amplitude response.
  • Separate conserved quantities, norms, and physical dissipation.
