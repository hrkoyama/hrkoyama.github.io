Introduction

This course introduces error, stability, boundary conditions, and time integration before you move into a particular application. It assumes elementary calculus and matrices, and explains the notation again within each chapter.

Start with the intuition, check the definitions and assumptions, and then work through the small calculations, code, and figures. Each review question has a short answer. On the Web, expand the answers to read them; the PDF displays those same answers in full.

We reuse a small set of one-dimensional problems and small matrices. The number of figures is not the number of independent physical validation problems. The course does not include new real-material calculations, large parameter sweeps, or performance studies of general multidimensional solvers.

The first coarse-grid sequence for the upwind method did not reach the prescribed observed order. We preserved its input and failed results and refined the grid sequence without changing the acceptance criterion. This also illustrates the relationship between observed and theoretical order.

Audience: Beginners who have studied elementary differentiation, integration, and matrices. No background in a particular branch of physics or engineering is assumed.

Run code snippets from the extracted ZIP directory. Enter the Chapter 19 commands in a shell rather than Python.

I Measuring error and reading results

  1. Approximation and the sources of error

    Distinguish absolute and relative error, roundoff, truncation, and iteration error; do not confuse displayed digits with accuracy.

  2. Error norms: L1, L2, and L∞

    Use continuous norms and grid-weighted discrete norms appropriately, and detect large localized errors.

  3. Residuals, conditioning, and backward error

    Separate sensitivity of the problem from stability of the algorithm, and understand why a small residual alone does not guarantee an accurate solution.

  4. Consistency, truncation error, and convergence order

    Distinguish local and global error, and investigate accuracy using Taylor expansions and refinement in space and time.

II Understanding stability and numerical instability

  1. Stability, convergence, and the Lax equivalence theorem

    For a well-posed linear initial-value problem and a consistent linear approximation, relate uniform stability to convergence in a specified norm.

  2. Absolute stability and stability regions

    Use the test equation y′=λy and amplification function R(λΔt) to relate the time step to growth and decay.

  3. A-stability, L-stability, and stiff problems

    Separate stability over the left half-plane from damping of very stiff modes; accuracy remains a separate question.

  4. von Neumann Fourier stability analysis

    Examine amplification by wavenumber, primarily for constant coefficients on a uniform grid with periodic or infinite domains.

  5. CFL conditions and domains of dependence

    Connect information speed to the reach of a stencil, and distinguish necessary from sufficient conditions.

  6. Numerical dissipation: amplitude, norms, and energy

    Read artificial amplitude decay from the magnitude of the amplification factor, distinguish it from physical diffusion and dissipation, and understand the role of artificial viscosity.

  7. Numerical dispersion: phase speed, group speed, and waveforms

    Use the argument of amplification to examine phase and propagation-speed error, understand waveform distortion without amplitude loss, and distinguish physical dispersion.

  8. Diagnosing numerical instability, oscillation, and divergence

    Distinguish error amplification and divergence from stable oscillations and physical instability, and isolate their causes.

III Working with initial and boundary conditions

  1. Initial conditions and Dirichlet, Neumann, and Robin boundaries

    Understand prescribed values, outward normal derivatives, and linear combinations of the two.

  2. Turning boundary conditions into discrete equations

    Use boundary nodes, half cells, ghost points, and normal directions to identify errors caused by low-order boundary closures.

  3. Well-posedness, pure-Neumann compatibility, and uniqueness

    Understand the arbitrary constant, integral compatibility, mean-value gauge, and boundary-inclusive stability of a pure-Neumann problem.

IV Combining methods and checking results

  1. The method of lines: separating space and time error

    Distinguish the spatially discrete ODE from the fully discrete system, and design comparisons that vary time or space separately.

  2. Lie-Trotter and Strang splitting

    Understand composition of subproblems and noncommutativity; distinguish first and second order under smooth conditions from boundary-induced order reduction.

  3. Symplectic integration and long-time behavior

    Introduce Hamiltonian phase space through the harmonic oscillator, and distinguish preservation of geometric structure from exact energy conservation.

  4. A common verification procedure using small problems

    Combine analytic and manufactured solutions, error norms, convergence, residuals, conserved quantities, energy budgets, and boundary error into one procedure.

Frequently used notation

h, Δx
Grid spacing. Also state whether the unknowns are located at nodes or cell centers.
Δt, n, T
Time step, step number, and the final time used for comparison.
u, U, e
Reference continuous solution, numerical solution, and their difference.
λ, z, R(z)
Linear growth or decay rate, the product λΔt, and the stability function of a time integrator.
k, θ, G(θ)
Continuous wavenumber, dimensionless grid wavenumber kh, and the Fourier amplification factor of a discretization.
κ(A)
Matrix condition number. Distinguish it from a thermal diffusivity also denoted by κ.
∂n, g
Outward normal derivative and prescribed boundary data.
q, p, H
Position, momentum, and Hamiltonian. Distinguish the momentum p from an error order also denoted by p.

Using the examples

Use Python 3.12 and only its standard library for the calculations. After extracting the shared code ZIP, run python verify_release.py from its directory. It reruns 25 calculation tests, 2 comparison tests, 34 fixed acceptance checks, and the comparison of 15 saved CSV files. No NumPy, network connection, randomness, or external solver is required.

To inspect a fresh run, use python lab.py --input inputs.json --output my-results with a new or empty my-results directory. Read the individual criteria in my-results/result.json and compare its CSV files with the figures. The archive README and some provenance records are in Japanese; the English chapters and these instructions explain how to run and interpret the same unchanged code.

The deliberately cancellation-sensitive derivative.csv is checked against an independent 80-digit Decimal Taylor reference and ULP-derived bounds, including checks of the floating-point sine and cosine assumptions at the fixed arguments. The other 14 CSV files retain relative tolerance 1e -9 and absolute tolerance 1e -10. This reproduction policy does not relax the original 34 scientific acceptance criteria. Regenerating plots additionally requires ReportLab 4.4.9 and Poppler; plot-byte equality is not required.

References and reproduction

Per-Olof Persson, Finite Difference Methods for PDEs

Relevant sections of the author's lecture notes were consulted for finite differences, consistency, uniform finite-time stability, the Lax equivalence theorem, and the method of lines for heat diffusion. This is distinct from reading the original research paper or auditing the complete notes.

T. A. Driscoll and R. J. Braun, Fundamentals of Numerical Computation: Stiffness

The relevant sections of the authors' textbook were consulted for semidiscrete diffusion, stiffness, and linearization.

Ernst Hairer, Lecture 2: Symplectic integrators (2010)

Pages 1-3 of the author's lecture-note PDF were consulted for the definition, symplectic Euler, Störmer-Verlet, composition, and separable systems. This does not claim an audit or reproduction of all long-time theorems or general nonlinear problems.

R. R. Rosales, Notes: von Neumann Stability Analysis (2022)

Provided as related reading already listed in the atlas. Earlier reading records are separate; we do not claim to have reread the original during preparation of this course.

Scope of the computations and production

Fixed small educational exercises. Intentional unstable/low-order controls and successful accurate-method checks have distinct meanings. Existing atlas scientific releases and their criteria are unchanged.
