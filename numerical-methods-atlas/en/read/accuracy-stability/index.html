A-stability, L-stability and error norms

A-stability and L-stability describe the response of a time integrator to linear decay modes. L1, L2 and L∞ instead describe the size and distribution of an error. They answer different questions. A method can be stable with a large error, and a small algebraic residual need not imply a small PDE error.

A/L-stability classification and accurate wave amplitudes and speeds are separate questions. For reading the magnitude and argument of an amplification factor, see von Neumann analysis: dissipation and dispersion basics.

A-stability concerns the whole left half-plane#

Apply a one-step method to y′=λy, with z=λΔt. Its stability function satisfies

yn+1=R(z)yn,S={z∈C:∣R(z)∣≤1}. y_{n+1}=R(z)y_n,\qquad \mathcal S=\{z\in\mathbb C:|R(z)|\leq1\}. yn+1​=R(z)yn​,S={z∈C:∣R(z)∣≤1}.

The method is A-stable if its absolute-stability region contains the entire closed left half-plane. In particular, R must have no pole there. Thus a scalar mode with Re λ≤0 does not grow for any positive step size in this test. Dahlquist (1963) is a foundational A-stability reference in the linear-multistep setting. The one-step notation here is used to make the comparison transparent.

Forward Euler, R(z)=1+z, is stable on the negative real axis only for −2≤z≤0. No consistent explicit RK method is A-stable: its nonconstant stability polynomial is unbounded along the negative real axis. Being implicit alone does not guarantee A-stability.

L-stability also damps infinitely stiff modes#

An L-stable one-step method is A-stable and satisfies

lim⁡∣z∣→∞,  Re⁡z<0R(z)=0. \lim_{|z|\to\infty,\;\operatorname{Re}z<0}R(z)=0. ∣z∣→∞,Rez<0lim​R(z)=0.

This extra property damps modes that are much faster than the chosen step. Backward Euler has R(z)=1/(1−z), so it is both A-stable and L-stable. Crank–Nicolson / the trapezoidal rule has

RCN(z)=1+z/21−z/2,lim⁡z→−∞RCN(z)=−1. R_{CN}(z)=\frac{1+z/2}{1-z/2},\qquad \lim_{z\to-\infty}R_{CN}(z)=-1. RCN​(z)=1−z/21+z/2​,z→−∞lim​RCN​(z)=−1.

It is A-stable but not L-stable: very stiff negative-real modes alternate sign and decay only slowly. Implicit midpoint has the same scalar linear R(z), although its nonlinear update is different from the trapezoidal rule. For z=−1000, backward Euler gives about 0.001 in one step, while CN gives about −0.996; the exact factor is exp(−1000).

For the implemented SDIRK2,

γ=1−1/2,R(z)=1+(1−2γ)z(1−γz)2. \gamma=1-1/\sqrt2,\qquad R(z)=\frac{1+(1-2\gamma)z}{(1-\gamma z)^2}. γ=1−1/2​,R(z)=(1−γz)21+(1−2γ)z​.

Its poles are in the right half-plane, it is A-stable and its large-z limit is zero. This is the L-stable two-stage family associated with Alexander (1977). BDF2 is a two-step method: analyze its characteristic roots and parasitic mode, rather than assigning it an arbitrary single-valued R(z).

These are linear absolute-stability statements. Accuracy, nonlinear contractivity, SSP/TVD inheritance, positivity and boundary stability require their own checks. Eigenvalues alone can also miss transient growth of nonnormal operators. An A-stable time step cannot repair an inconsistent spatial discretization, and L-stability cannot make an underresolved transient accurate.

Continuous L1, L2 and L∞ errors#

For scalar error e(x)=u_h(x)−u_ref(x) on a domain Ω,

∥e∥L1=∫Ω∣e∣ dV,∥e∥L2=(∫Ω∣e∣2 dV)1/2,∥e∥L∞=ess sup⁡x∈Ω∣e(x)∣. \|e\|_{L^1}=\int_\Omega |e|\,dV,\qquad \|e\|_{L^2}=\left(\int_\Omega |e|^2\,dV\right)^{1/2},\qquad \|e\|_{L^\infty}=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{x\in\Omega}|e(x)|. ∥e∥L1​=∫Ω​∣e∣dV,∥e∥L2​=(∫Ω​∣e∣2dV)1/2,∥e∥L∞​=x∈Ωesssup​∣e(x)∣.

L1 measures integrated absolute error. L2 emphasizes larger deviations more strongly. L∞ measures the worst error apart from measure-zero sets. For a continuous error on a compact domain, the essential supremum equals the maximum. For discontinuous solutions, the value assigned exactly at a jump does not change the continuous norm.

For a domain with volume V, mean absolute error is L1/V and RMS error is L2/√V. These normalized quantities have the same physical units as u; unnormalized integral norms also contain powers of length. State the convention before comparing domains of different size.

Discrete weights and the reference representation#

For finite-volume error e_i between a numerical cell mean and a reference cell mean, with positive cell volumes V_i,

E1=∑iVi∣ei∣,E2=(∑iVi∣ei∣2)1/2,E∞=max⁡i∣ei∣. E_1=\sum_iV_i|e_i|,\qquad E_2=\left(\sum_iV_i|e_i|^2\right)^{1/2},\qquad E_\infty=\max_i|e_i|. E1​=i∑​Vi​∣ei​∣,E2​=(i∑​Vi​∣ei​∣2)1/2,E∞​=imax​∣ei​∣.

On a uniform grid, V_i=h^d. On the unit interval, these become mean absolute error, RMS error and maximum cell-mean error. On a nonuniform mesh, an unweighted arithmetic mean generally gives the wrong spatial weighting. For e=(1,3) and cell volumes (0.75,0.25), E1=1.5, E2=√3 and E∞=3; unweighted means disagree.

The Euclidean vector norm np.linalg.norm(e) omits the h^(d/2) factor of a physical discrete L2 norm. Comparing that raw norm across meshes can therefore change an apparent convergence exponent. Relative error E_p/||u_ref||_p needs a stated reference norm; when the denominator is zero, it is undefined. Any regularization must be explicitly reported.

A reference at cell centers is not an exact cell-average reference. DG and FEM need reconstruction and quadrature inside elements; a coefficient-vector norm is not automatically a function L2 norm. Likewise, a maximum over sample points is a sampled L∞ diagnostic, not necessarily the exact supremum of a high-order reconstructed field. Vector components with different units should be reported separately or nondimensionalized with stated scales.

Convergence, shocks and algebraic residuals#

For matched runs at the same physical time and error convention, the observed order is

pobs=log⁡(Ecoarse/Efine)log⁡(hcoarse/hfine). p_{obs}=\frac{\log(E_{coarse}/E_{fine})}{\log(h_{coarse}/h_{fine})}. pobs​=log(hcoarse​/hfine​)log(Ecoarse​/Efine​)​.

Temporal error, reference error, quadrature error, roundoff and incomplete iterative convergence can mask spatial order. Refine time separately, or state how Δt scales with h. Adaptive ODE tolerance is a local, component-scaled error control; it is not a global spatial L2 error bound.

Different norms can behave differently near shocks. For example, an O(1) error supported on width O(h) has L1=O(h), L2=O(√h) and L∞=O(1). This simple scaling is not a universal convergence-rate claim for a shock solver. A useful L1 refinement result need not imply maximum-norm convergence of a shifted jump.

For Ax=b, the residual is r=b−Ax. The solution error satisfies x_num−x_exact=−A^(−1)r, so conditioning matters. An algebraic residual compares against the discrete equation; discretization error compares that equation's solution against the continuous problem. See sparse/nonlinear solvers and the Lax–Richtmyer discussion.

Where the atlas reports each quantity#

  • The equation gallery reports L1/MAE, L2/RMS and L∞ over its stored unit-interval profiles. Its labels retain the distinction between exact cell means, point-sampled Euler references and absent references.
  • The browser stability laboratory has a point-sampled L1/MAE diagnostic and amplification factors; it does not thereby measure a reconstructed finite-volume function norm.
  • P1 FEM integrates L2 and H1 seminorm errors inside triangles.
  • Periodic MAC flow reports a combined velocity RMS error and maximum absolute discrete divergence. These are different diagnostics.
  • Stiff integration reports maximum error over accepted time nodes, separate from nonlinear stage residuals and local step estimates.

Original exhibits keep their documented conventions; this chapter does not retroactively relabel point samples as cell averages or certify a missing maximum-norm calculation.
