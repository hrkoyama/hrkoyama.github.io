Differences, CFL, von Neumann analysis, and Lax

Try the interactive stability laboratory and read the experiment guide to connect the formulas below with live browser calculations.

Choose space and time discretizations separately#

Forward, backward, and centered spatial differences are

D+uj=uj+1−ujh,D−uj=uj−uj−1h,D0uj=uj+1−uj−12h. D_+u_j=\frac{u_{j+1}-u_j}{h},\qquad D_-u_j=\frac{u_j-u_{j-1}}h,\qquad D_0u_j=\frac{u_{j+1}-u_{j-1}}{2h}. D+​uj​=huj+1​−uj​​,D−​uj​=huj​−uj−1​​,D0​uj​=2huj+1​−uj−1​​.

For smooth functions, the first two are first-order accurate and the centered stencil is second order. Compare them with numart.advection.finite_difference. For linear advection u_t+a u_x=0, upwinding follows information propagation: use a backward difference for a>0 and a forward difference for a<0. A forward difference is therefore not synonymous with an upwind difference.

Forward Euler in time gives u^{n+1}=u^n+Δt L_h u^n; backward Euler gives u^{n+1}=u^n+Δt L_h u^{n+1} and requires a system solve. A backward spatial stencil alone does not make a method implicit.

CFL: can the stencil reach the information it needs?#

For constant-coefficient periodic advection with an explicit nearest-neighbor stencil, domain-of-dependence reasoning requires |a|Δt/h ≤ 1. The constant changes with the stencil, Runge–Kutta method, DG degree, and dimension. Explicit diffusion introduces a Δt=O(h²) restriction. A CFL condition is generally necessary rather than sufficient for stability. Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (1928).

Forward-time, centered-space (FTCS) advection is a counterexample. Substituting a Fourier mode u_j^n=G^n e^{ijθ} gives

G=1−iCsin⁡θ,∣G∣2=1+C2sin⁡2θ,C=aΔt/h. G=1-i C\sin θ,\quad |G|^2=1+C^2\sin^2 θ, \qquad C=a\Delta t/h. G=1−iCsinθ,∣G∣2=1+C2sin2θ,C=aΔt/h.

Some modes grow even when |C|<1. The scheme is unstable under ordinary fixed-CFL grid refinement.

Lax–Friedrichs and Lax–Wendroff#

The classical Lax–Friedrichs scheme is

ujn+1=12(uj+1n+uj−1n)−C2(uj+1n−uj−1n),G=cos⁡θ−iCsin⁡θ. u_j^{n+1}=\tfrac12(u_{j+1}^n+u_{j-1}^n) -\tfrac C2(u_{j+1}^n-u_{j-1}^n),\qquad G=\cos θ-iC\sin θ. ujn+1​=21​(uj+1n​+uj−1n​)−2C​(uj+1n​−uj−1n​),G=cosθ−iCsinθ.

Since |G|²=1-(1-C²)sin²θ, periodic linear advection is stable for |C|≤1. Classical LF uses the numerical-viscosity coefficient h/Δt. Rusanov uses a local maximum wave speed; WENO here uses global LF flux splitting. These are different discrete constructions. Lax (1954).

Lax–Wendroff is

ujn+1=ujn−C2(uj+1n−uj−1n)+C22(uj+1n−2ujn+uj−1n), u_j^{n+1}=u_j^n-\tfrac C2(u_{j+1}^n-u_{j-1}^n) +\tfrac{C^2}{2}(u_{j+1}^n-2u_j^n+u_{j-1}^n), ujn+1​=ujn​−2C​(uj+1n​−uj−1n​)+2C2​(uj+1n​−2ujn​+uj−1n​),
G=1−iCsin⁡θ+C2(cos⁡θ−1),∣G∣2=1−4C2(1−C2)sin⁡4(θ/2). G=1-iC\sin θ+C^2(\cos θ-1),\quad |G|^2=1-4C^2(1-C^2)\sin^4(θ/2). G=1−iCsinθ+C2(cosθ−1),∣G∣2=1−4C2(1−C2)sin4(θ/2).

Its stability condition for this problem is also |C|≤1. Second-order accuracy does not itself suppress oscillations near discontinuities. Lax–Wendroff (1960).

Dissipation and dispersion: read amplitude and phase separately#

The same amplification factor used to test growth also explains two distinct errors: numerical dissipation damps a wave's amplitude; numerical dispersion changes its propagation speed in a wavelength-dependent way. Consider constant-speed scalar advection on a periodic uniform grid. Let h be the grid spacing, Δt the positive time step, k the wavenumber, θ the dimensionless grid wavenumber, and C the signed Courant number. The Fourier convention and exact one-step factor are

ut+aux=0,xj=jh,θ=kh,C=aΔth,ujn=u^ neijθ,Gexact=e−iakΔt=e−iCθ.\begin{gathered}u_t+a u_x=0,\qquad x_j=jh,\quad\theta=kh,\quad C=\frac{a\Delta t}{h},\\u_j^n=\widehat u^{\,n}e^{ij\theta},\qquad G_{\rm exact}=e^{-ia k\Delta t}=e^{-iC\theta}.\end{gathered}ut​+aux​=0,xj​=jh,θ=kh,C=haΔt​,ujn​=uneijθ,Gexact​=e−iakΔt=e−iCθ.​

The exact solution translates without changing amplitude. This makes the model useful for separating artificial damping from transport error.

What the amplification factor measures#

For a linear, translation-invariant one-step scheme, write its factor in polar form:

G(θ)=∣G(θ)∣e−iϕ(θ),u^ n=G(θ)nu^ 0,∣u^ n∣∣u^ 0∣=∣G(θ)∣n.\begin{aligned}G(\theta)&=|G(\theta)|e^{-i\phi(\theta)},\\\widehat u^{\,n}&=G(\theta)^n\widehat u^{\,0},\qquad\frac{|\widehat u^{\,n}|}{|\widehat u^{\,0}|}=|G(\theta)|^n.\end{aligned}G(θ)un​=∣G(θ)∣e−iϕ(θ),=G(θ)nu0,∣u0∣∣un∣​=∣G(θ)∣n.​

Thus |G|<1 damps this mode; repeated steps accumulate the loss. |G|=1 preserves its amplitude, while |G|>1 amplifies it. For this scalar periodic problem, the familiar requirement |G|≤1 controls growth, but says nothing about whether the amplitude stays close to its exact value of one.

For 0<|G|, the loss can also be expressed as a damping rate σnum per unit time:

σnum=−log⁡∣G∣Δt,∣u^ n∣=e−σnumtn∣u^ 0∣,tn=nΔt.\begin{aligned}\sigma_{\rm num}&=-\frac{\log|G|}{\Delta t},\\|\widehat u^{\,n}|&=e^{-\sigma_{\rm num}t_n}|\widehat u^{\,0}|,\qquad t_n=n\Delta t.\end{aligned}σnum​∣un∣​=−Δtlog∣G∣​,=e−σnum​tn​∣u0∣,tn​=nΔt.​

This is a decay rate with units of inverse time, not a diffusion coefficient. At G=0 the mode vanishes in one step; a finite damping rate and phase are undefined.

Phase needs a sign convention as well. Use arg₀ for an argument continued from G(0)=1 along a branch on which G never vanishes. The minus sign matches the exact factor above:

ϕ(θ)=−arg⁡0G(θ),ωnum=ϕ(θ)Δt,cnuma=ωnum/ka=ϕ(θ)Cθ(ak≠0).\begin{aligned}\phi(\theta)&=-\arg_0G(\theta),\qquad\omega_{\rm num}=\frac{\phi(\theta)}{\Delta t},\\\frac{c_{\rm num}}a&=\frac{\omega_{\rm num}/k}a=\frac{\phi(\theta)}{C\theta}\qquad(ak\ne0).\end{aligned}ϕ(θ)acnum​​​=−arg0​G(θ),ωnum​=Δtϕ(θ)​,=aωnum​/k​=Cθϕ(θ)​(ak=0).​

Correct transport requires φ=Cθ, as well as |G|=1. A stable wave can travel at the wrong speed and accumulate a large position error. At θ=0, use the low-wavenumber limit for the speed ratio. At G=0 the scheme has removed the mode and its phase is undefined. Principal arguments wrap by 2π; treating that jump as a physical change gives a false dispersion curve. At the Nyquist endpoints θ=±π, the sampled modes coincide, so their propagation direction cannot be uniquely recovered from grid samples.

Three examples reveal different errors#

For a>0, forward Euler with backward spatial differencing is the first-order upwind scheme. Direct substitution gives

Gup=1−C(1−e−iθ),∣Gup∣2=1−4C(1−C)sin⁡2 ⁣(θ2),0≤C≤1(a>0).\begin{aligned}G_{\rm up}&=1-C(1-e^{-i\theta}),\\|G_{\rm up}|^2&=1-4C(1-C)\sin^2\!\left(\frac\theta2\right),\\&\hspace{1em}0\le C\le1\qquad(a>0).\end{aligned}Gup​∣Gup​∣2​=1−C(1−e−iθ),=1−4C(1−C)sin2(2θ​),0≤C≤1(a>0).​

For 0<C<1, every nonconstant resolved Fourier mode is damped. This is numerical dissipation: the underlying advection equation has no physical decay. Its phase generally also differs from Cθ. For a<0, reverse the upwind stencil.

Centered differencing with time still continuous instead gives

dujdt=−auj+1−uj−12h,λh=−iahsin⁡θ,ωh=ahsin⁡θ,cha=sin⁡θθ.\begin{aligned}\frac{d u_j}{dt}&=-a\frac{u_{j+1}-u_{j-1}}{2h},\qquad\lambda_h=-i\frac ah\sin\theta,\\\omega_h&=\frac ah\sin\theta,\qquad\frac{c_h}a=\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta}.\end{aligned}dtduj​​ωh​​=−a2huj+1​−uj−1​​,λh​=−iha​sinθ,=ha​sinθ,ach​​=θsinθ​.​

The eigenvalue is purely imaginary, so this semidiscrete mode has no amplitude decay. Yet sinθ/θ differs from one: it is dispersive. For θ=π/2, the phase speed is only 2/π≈0.637 times a. At θ=π the centered derivative vanishes, a stationary Nyquist checkerboard annihilated by the centered derivative, rather than correct transport.

Time discretization changes the result. With forward Euler,

GFE+centered=1−iCsin⁡θ,∣GFE+centered∣2=1+C2sin⁡2θ.\begin{aligned}G_{\rm FE+centered}&=1-iC\sin\theta,\\|G_{\rm FE+centered}|^2&=1+C^2\sin^2\theta.\end{aligned}GFE+centered​∣GFE+centered​∣2​=1−iCsinθ,=1+C2sin2θ.​

Modes with sinθ≠0 grow for any nonzero C, explaining the FTCS instability discussed above. Other time integrators have different amplitude and phase responses on the same spatial eigenvalues. The linearized centered-wave discussion in KEEP and its RK4 amplitude and phase analysis make this separation explicit.

For Lax–Wendroff (1960), the factor already derived above also yields

GLW=1−iCsin⁡θ+C2(cos⁡θ−1),∣GLW∣2=1−4C2(1−C2)sin⁡4 ⁣(θ2).\begin{aligned}G_{\rm LW}&=1-iC\sin\theta+C^2(\cos\theta-1),\\|G_{\rm LW}|^2&=1-4C^2(1-C^2)\sin^4\!\left(\frac\theta2\right).\end{aligned}GLW​∣GLW​∣2​=1−iCsinθ+C2(cosθ−1),=1−4C2(1−C2)sin4(2θ​).​

At generic 0<|C|<1 it therefore has amplitude error as well as phase error; calling it “purely dispersive” is incorrect. Its leading smooth-wave error can be dispersive without eliminating higher-order damping. At C=1, both upwind and Lax–Wendroff reduce to G=e−iθ, an exact one-cell shift on this grid. This special Courant number is not representative of other step sizes.

For C=0.8 and θ=π/2 (four grid spacings per wavelength), the one-step amplitude gain is about 0.8246 for upwind and 0.8773 for Lax–Wendroff. Their phase-speed ratios are about 1.0551 and 0.9135, respectively: the first travels too fast and the second too slowly. Both are stable, yet both have amplitude and phase errors.

Keep the physical and numerical questions distinct#

If the physical PDE includes viscosity or another decay mechanism, the exact |G| can itself be below one for the mode considered; compare numerical damping with that exact decay. Likewise, a physically dispersive PDE has a wavenumber-dependent exact phase speed. Error means disagreement with the physical reference.

A-stability and L-stability classify a time integrator's response to linear test modes; neither guarantees accurate wave propagation at a chosen resolution. The formulas here concern scalar periodic linear advection. They do not certify nonlinear Euler dynamics, shocks, or wall boundary treatments.

A useful diagnostic first fixes h and reduces Δt to expose the time integrator's contribution. Then reduce h with temporal error controlled. Refining with C held fixed changes both scales and does not by itself separate the two errors. For a smooth single mode, compare amplitude and unwrapped phase at the same physical time; agreement in only one of these quantities is incomplete evidence of accuracy.

The formulas and worked values here follow by directly substituting Fourier modes into the displayed differences. For the assumptions of the analysis and comparison against the exact amplification factor, see R. R. Rosales’s author lecture notes (2022), §1 and §1.1.2.

The Lax–Richtmyer equivalence theorem#

Consider a well-posed linear initial-value problem in an appropriate Banach space and a consistent linear difference approximation. When stability means uniform boundedness of the discrete evolution operators over a fixed finite time interval, stability is equivalent to convergence. Lax–Richtmyer (1956), original paper.

Writing the error as e^{n+1}=S_h e^n+Δt τ^n, the bound ||S_h^m||≤M_T gives ||e^n||≤M_T||e^0||+M_T T max||τ^k||. Consistency makes the truncation-error term tend to zero, providing the basic stability-to-convergence argument. The converse requires a uniform-boundedness argument.

The theorem does not directly certify nonlinear Euler, WENO, shocks, or arbitrary boundary treatments. The Fourier criterion |G|≤1 used here applies to periodic, constant-coefficient scalar problems. Boundaries, variable coefficients, nonnormal matrices, and multistep schemes need additional analysis.

Time steps in the first edition#

  • Euler and barotropic systems: Δt=C h/max(|u|+c), with default C=0.3.
  • DG: Δt=C h/((2p+1)|a|), with default C=0.2.
  • 2D MHD: Δt=C/[max(s_x)/h+max(s_y)/h], with default C=0.3.

These conservative choices are checked on selected examples. They are not rigorous nonlinear stability limits for every initial condition.

For definitions and comparisons of A-stability, L-stability and L1/L2/L∞ error norms, see the shared accuracy and stability guide.
